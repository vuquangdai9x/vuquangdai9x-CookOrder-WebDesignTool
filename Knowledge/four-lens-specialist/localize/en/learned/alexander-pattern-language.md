# A Pattern Language — Learned Notes

Notes below are paraphrased from my own reading of the OCR'd text of the 1977 edition (Oxford University Press). The book contains 253 numbered "patterns," ordered largest-to-smallest: regions and towns, then neighborhoods and clusters, then individual buildings, then rooms, then construction details. Each pattern follows a fixed format: a picture, a linking paragraph to "larger" patterns it helps complete, a problem statement backed by evidence/observation, a boldface solution stated as an instruction, a diagram, and a closing paragraph linking to "smaller" patterns that complete it. Patterns are marked with two asterisks (believed to be a near-universal invariant), one asterisk (a good but improvable solution), or no asterisk (one possible solution among many, offered provisionally). No pattern stands alone — each exists embedded in the larger patterns above it and is completed by the smaller ones below it, which is the core structural idea worth borrowing for level design: a level is not a list of set-pieces but a nested web of spaces that reinforce each other.

## Towns and Neighborhoods (Patterns 1–94)

This first third of the book deals with regional and city-scale structure: mosaic of subcultures, community-scale governance units, transportation networks, local centers, housing clusters, work communities, informal road networks, public open land, common land, and finally the smallest social institutions (family, workgroups, shops). Most of it operates at a scale larger than a single game level (city planning policy, transit networks), but several patterns describe recurring human-scale phenomena — thresholds of walkable distance, the pull of landmarks, the psychology of public squares — that translate directly into open-world zone and hub design.

### Gradients of Publicness and Neighborhood Character (Pattern 36)
People split along an "extrovert/introvert" axis in how close to activity they want to live: some want to be near the action, some want maximum isolation, most want something in between. A cited local survey found roughly 20% of households wanted to live within a few blocks of a commercial center, 60% wanted a middle distance, and 20% wanted to be well away from it. The pattern's instruction is that any cluster of houses (or, for a game, any cluster of activity) should offer three genuinely different flavors of address — near-the-action, in-between, and remote — connected by three correspondingly different kinds of paths: busy/wide paths near services, narrow/twisting low-traffic paths in the quiet zones, and intermediate connectors between them. For level design, this argues for zoning a hub not just by function but by a felt "volume" gradient, and giving each zone a path character (width, straightness, traffic density) that matches its social temperature rather than uniform corridors everywhere.

### Landmarks, Gateways, and Orientation (Patterns 53, 62)
**Main Gateways** should be built as solid, deliberately noticeable "things" — visible from every approach, sometimes literally punched through a building or crossing a change of level — with the transition itself emphasized through a shift in light, surface, or elevation as the person passes through. A gateway is treated as the starting point of circulation inside whatever precinct it guards, not just a boundary marker.

**High Places** answers what the text frames as a near-universal human instinct: the urge to climb to a vantage point and look down over your surroundings, and conversely to have a landmark you can orient toward from a distance. The pattern notes that even small hamlets tend to produce a dominating landmark (a church tower, a hilltop), and that these landmarks serve two complementary functions — a place to climb to and see from, and a place to be seen and navigate toward from elsewhere. This is essentially the architectural version of a game's "read the landmark from anywhere in the zone" wayfinding principle, arrived at independently of games.

### Public Greens and Small Squares: Human-Scale Thresholds (Patterns 60, 61, 123)
**Accessible Green** is built on empirical distance-decay data: park usage drops sharply beyond roughly two to three blocks (about a three-minute walk, ~750 feet) from home or workplace — the fall-off follows a curve that is gentle at first, then falls off a cliff at a specific threshold, then flattens. The book's instruction is to guarantee a public green within three minutes' walk of every house and workplace, sized large enough (very roughly 60,000 sq ft, at least 150 ft in the narrow dimension) that the middle of it actually feels like a break from the built environment. The general lesson for level design is that "accessibility" isn't a binary in/out of range — it decays sharply past a specific threshold distance, so rest/social spaces need to be seeded at that walkable interval, not just "somewhere in the zone."

**Small Public Squares** is grounded in measured limits of face-to-face perception: two people can comfortably talk up to about 75 feet apart; beyond 100 feet conversation and facial expression reading become hopeless. The instruction: keep a square's short dimension to 45–70 feet, and more precisely size it to the expected number of occupants (roughly 150–300 square feet per person) rather than making it impressively large. Pattern 123, **Pedestrian Density**, generalizes this with field observations from San Francisco plazas: around 150 sq ft per person reads as "alive," and above roughly 500 sq ft per person a plaza reads as dead no matter how nicely paved. This is a directly portable rule for hub/plaza sizing in games — oversized gathering spaces feel empty even when populated, because density (not raw population) drives the "alive" perception.

### Remaining Town-Scale Patterns (grouped)
- **Regional/city structure** (1–20): regional policy, city character as a mosaic of subcultures, scattered work, transit webs, ring roads, self-governing community units (~7000 people) and neighborhoods with real edges (subculture boundary, neighborhood boundary).
- **Local character policy** (21–27): height limits, parking ratios, road patterns, sacred sites, water access, life-cycle housing mix.
- **Local centers** (28–34): eccentric (off-center) nucleus placement, density rings around centers, activity nodes, promenades, shopping streets, night life zones, transit interchanges.
- **Housing clusters** (35–40): household mix, the publicness gradient (36, detailed above), house clusters as face-to-face groups, row houses, hillside housing, integrating the elderly.
- **Work communities** (41–48): mixed work/live zones, markets, health centers, local town halls, community-project "necklaces."
- **Informal road/path networks** (49–57): looped local roads, T-junctions (to kill through-traffic), green streets, mixed paths-and-cars networks, gateways (53, detailed above), road crossings raised for pedestrian right-of-way, raised walks, bike infrastructure, child-safe streets.
- **Public open land** (58–66): carnival/release-valve space, quiet backs, accessible green (60, detailed above), small public squares (61, detailed above), high places (62, detailed above), street dancing, water features, birth/death/sacred ground.
- **Common land and smallest institutions** (67–94): shared local land, connected play, family structures and housing for different household types, self-governing workshops, teenage society, shopfront schools, individually owned shops, street cafés, corner groceries, travelers' inns, bus stops, food stands.

## Buildings and the Space Between (Patterns 95–204)

This middle section is the most transferable to game level design: it moves from siting a cluster of buildings, to positioning individual buildings on their site, to shaping entrances and gardens, to laying out paths and squares between buildings, to the internal "gradients" of a building's rooms, down to individual alcoves and windows. Alexander repeatedly insists that indoor space and outdoor space must be shaped together — the solid and the void are treated as equally designed "figure" and "ground," which maps closely onto how a level's built mass and its negative/open space should be co-designed rather than one carved out of the other as an afterthought.

### Site Repair and Positive Outdoor Space (Patterns 104, 106)
**Site Repair** makes an argument that runs against instinct: build on the *worst* part of a site (the swampy corner, the dark scrap of land, the awkward slope), not the best. Building on the healthiest, most scenic part of a site both destroys what was good there and leaves the already-poor remainder to degrade further unused. For a game world, this reframes "prime real estate" placement — put the town/POI where it repairs a dead or ugly part of the map's ecology, and preserve the naturally striking terrain as untouched space instead of a building footprint.

**Positive Outdoor Space** argues that people instinctively avoid sitting exposed in the open middle of a space — they gravitate to partial enclosure (a tree to put their back against, a hollow, a recess), citing park-use studies showing people seek areas that are "partly enclosed and partly open," never fully exposed and never fully boxed in. The design instruction is to make every outdoor space between buildings a positive, convex, partially-enclosed shape (using wings of buildings, trees, hedges, walls) rather than leftover, shapeless L-shaped voids that spill around corners indefinitely. This is essentially the architectural articulation of "positive space" vs. "negative space" in level layout — the gaps between structures need the same deliberate shaping as the structures themselves, or players will feel adrift in them the way pedestrians avoid dead plazas.

### Entrances, Gateways, and Transitions (Patterns 110, 111, 112)
**Main Entrance** should be visible from the main lines of approach even at an acute angle — achieved by having the entrance physically project past the building line, and/or by raising the building's height locally around the entrance so it reads from a distance. The entrance should be strongly differentiated from its surroundings by shape, not just signage.

**Half-Hidden Garden** solves a placement paradox: a garden placed fully at the front (facing the street) becomes decorative and unused because it isn't private enough; a garden placed fully at the back becomes isolated and unused because it loses all connection to the street's life and sense of other people nearby. The pattern's answer is to place gardens to the *side*, half-hidden — private enough to relax in, but with a sightline (through a gate, a gap in a wall) back to the street/entrance so the space never feels fully cut off. This is a strong, transferable idea for optional/rest areas in a level: a secluded space that still keeps a thin visual or audio thread back to the main path reads as inviting; one that's either too exposed or too sealed off goes unused.

**Entrance Transition** is possibly the single most game-relevant pattern in the whole book: it argues that people carry "street behavior" (tense, guarded, public-facing) with them, and cannot relax into a building's more intimate register unless something breaks that momentum during the passage inward — a change of light, level, surface, or view. It cites a study of a public exhibit that failed to hold visitors' attention until a bright, deep-pile orange carpet was placed at the entrance; crossing it functioned to "wipe clean" people's outside-world mode and let them engage with what was inside, even though nothing else about the exhibit changed. A related study found that houses whose entrances had more transitional stages (a long gallery, a shift of light, a view into the distance) were rated as more "houselike." This is a direct architectural precedent for the game-design instinct to use load corridors, gate rooms, changes in lighting/ambient sound, or a literal threshold object to reset player state between a public hub and a private/high-stakes space.

### Paths, Arcades, and the Edges of Public Space (Patterns 119–126, 160)
**Arcades** — covered walkways set partly inside, partly outside a building — are held up as one of the most powerful patterns in the language because they create *ambiguous* territory between a building's private interior and the fully public street, drawing people in without committing them to enter. Requirements given: the arcade must be at least ~7 feet deep and covered so it reads as an extension of the interior, and its ceiling edge must stay low or the ambiguous, sheltering feeling collapses.

**Paths and Goals** and **Path Shape** describe how people actually walk: rather than continuously recalculating a bearing, walkers pick a visible intermediate "goal" (a landmark, gateway, tree, statue) and walk toward it in a rough straight line, re-picking a new goal every hundred yards or so as it comes into view — producing a slow curve rather than a straight line when goals keep shifting. The practical instruction: lay paths out by first placing goals at natural points of interest, then connecting them, keeping goals no more than a few hundred feet apart, and letting the path itself bulge/widen near a goal rather than staying a uniform ribbon. Path Shape adds that streets/paths should be subtly convex (bulging) at points meant for lingering, turning a through-route into an occasional stopping place — this is close to modern level-design "breadcrumbing" and gives it an empirical, human-perception basis rather than a purely gameplay-pacing one.

**Building Fronts, Pedestrian Density (123, quantified above), Activity Pockets,** and **Stair Seats** together describe how the *edge* of a public space, not its open middle, is what makes it feel alive. Activity Pockets shows that people gravitate to a space's edge and will not linger in the open middle unless the edge is "scalloped" with alcoves, shops, benches, and other pause-points that alternate with clear paths through them — and that this scalloping must wrap the entire perimeter, since the area a space can "cover" socially collapses sharply once the active edge shrinks below covering the whole boundary. Stair Seats notes that the most inviting spots in a space are ones that are simultaneously slightly elevated (a vantage point) and easily reachable from the main flow — hence the enduring appeal of low, wide steps as informal seating at plaza edges.

**Something Roughly in the Middle** observes that a public space with a wholly empty center reads as unfinished no matter how well the edges are handled, citing the near-universal instinct to place *something* — a fountain, statue, tree, market stall — off-center at the natural crossing point of paths through the space (deliberately *not* dead-center, which the text calls a modern "affliction" rather than a genuine pattern).

**Building Edge** generalizes the same edge-preference to the building/street boundary itself: citing Jan Gehl's pedestrian-behavior observations that people strongly prefer standing/sitting near a facade, pillar, or piece of furniture rather than out in open space, the pattern argues a building's exterior wall should be treated as a thick, occupiable zone (niches, benches, alcoves up to ~6 ft deep) rather than a flat plane — an unadorned flat facade produces a "dead" no-man's-land around the building that isolates it from the life of the street.

### The Intimacy Gradient and Interior Flow (Patterns 127, 129, 131–133)
**Intimacy Gradient** is the pattern most directly reusable as a level-design vocabulary term: it argues every building needs rooms sequenced by degree of privacy, from most public (entrance/porch) through progressively more private zones (common areas, then bedrooms/private rooms), so that where a visitor is physically standing communicates, without words, how much intimacy/trust the relationship has reached. A detailed Peruvian house example shows guests self-sorting at a fiesta: casual acquaintances never get past the front room, closer friends reach the family room, only intimates enter the kitchen or bedrooms — everyone reads the gradient without being told the rules. The instruction generalizes to offices and shops too (entry lobby → workspaces → private lounge; shop entrance → browsing area → sales counter → staff-only). This maps directly onto quest-gating, faction-trust areas, or dungeon depth in games: physical depth into a space should correlate with narrative/social access level.

**Common Areas at the Heart** specifies exactly how a shared gathering space should sit relative to circulation, based on the finding that a common room works only if daily paths run *tangent* to it — passing close enough that people naturally glance in and can join on impulse — rather than dead-ending at it (too easy to ignore) or cutting straight through its middle (too exposed to ever settle into). This tangent-path principle is a clean, portable rule for placing social hubs, camps, or NPC gathering points along a route rather than off it.

**Flow Through Rooms, Short Passages,** and **Staircase as a Stage** address connective tissue: rooms should connect via generous, light-filled loops rather than dead corridors; unavoidable passages need light, furnishing, and — per a cited hospital-corridor study on disorientation and anxiety in long, uniform corridors — should be kept under roughly 50 feet before they start reading as dead/monotonous, after which they need a break in scale (a widening, a change of light) to stay legible. Staircase as a Stage treats stairs as social theater, not just vertical transit: stairs open to the room below (rather than boxed in a stairwell), with wide, flared bottom steps that double as seating, become a place for entrances, observation, and being observed simultaneously — directly useful for grand-hall or throne-room staging in games.

### Light as Navigation and Mood (Patterns 134, 135, 159, 252)
**Zen View** is built around a single vivid example: a mountain monk's house whose courtyard wall has one narrow diagonal slit, positioned so that a person crossing the courtyard sees the distant ocean for a single instant, then loses it again on the far side. The book's claim is that a view kept perpetually visible through a huge window eventually fades into unnoticed wallpaper, while a glimpse rationed out at a single point of transition stays powerful indefinitely — so significant views should be framed at thresholds and passage-points (stairs, hallways, gateways) rather than thrown wide open from a room where people linger.

**Tapestry of Light and Dark** and **Pools of Light** together describe light as a navigation signal, not just illumination: people are described as "phototropic" — they move toward brighter areas even when stationary, orienting themselves toward light — so entrances and circulation nodes must be the *brightest* points in a building, or foot traffic will drift toward whatever happens to be lit instead and get lost/frustrated. Pools of Light adds a small empirical study (a Berkeley lounge where 13 of 21 observed occupants chose seats next to lamps, out of 42 total seats, only 12 of them lamp-adjacent) to argue for deliberately uneven lighting — separate pools of warm light over gathering points (tables, chairs) with genuine darkness between them — rather than flat, even illumination, because uniform light produces a space with no legible "places" at all.

**Light on Two Sides of Every Room** (noted more briefly) argues rooms lit from only one side read as flat and glary, while light entering from two directions reveals volume and texture — a useful principle for how to lay out windows/light sources in an interior level so geometry reads clearly rather than washing out.

### Windows, Views, and Small Gathering Places (Patterns 164, 171, 180, 185, 192, 241)
**Street Windows** notes upper-floor windows (2nd–3rd story) sustain a lively two-way connection to street life (people can call down, glimpse faces) that ground-floor windows struggle to replicate without a compromise like a raised alcove sill.

**Tree Places** rejects treating trees as instrumentally-placed landscaping objects and instead catalogs the archetypal social geometries a tree (or trees) can produce: a single low, spreading tree as an "umbrella" room; two trees forming a gateway; several trees as a grove; a ring of trees enclosing a square; a double row lining a path as an avenue. The pattern's point is that a tree only "means something" to people once it actually forms one of these place-types, not when it's dropped in a tub as decoration.

**Window Place** catalogs specific dimensioned variants of a window nook (bay window, simple window seat, low-sill armchair spot at 12–14 inches, glazed alcove) and insists any frequently-used room deserves at least one such place, treated as real volume rather than "a hole in the wall."

**Sitting Circle** specifies the geometry of a group seating arrangement that actually gets used: a loose, roughly circular (never a strict circle) cluster, protected from cutting paths, anchored by something like a fireplace, with intentionally one or two more chairs than needed — the slight surplus and looseness is called out as what keeps the group animated (people can get up, move, rejoin) rather than a rigid formal furniture layout.

**Windows Overlooking Life** cites office-worker studies (Manning; Markus) finding that people strongly prefer windows with "meaningful" views of activity (city life, gardens) over views that are merely large but visually empty, and that people near windows over-estimate how much daylight far-from-window occupants get — suggesting the preference is really about the view's content, not the light itself. It gives a rough regional benchmark (~25% window area to floor area in the Bay Area climate) as a sizing heuristic.

**Seat Spots** (light coverage) reports a small Berkeley field survey of outdoor benches: of eleven observed, three were occupied and eight empty, and every occupied bench simultaneously had a view of activity, direct sun, and low wind — while none of the empty benches had all three conditions at once. This is a compact, quotable rule for any placed "sit here" prompt in a game world: a rest spot needs sightline + comfort + shelter together, not any one alone.

### Remaining Building-Scale Patterns (grouped)
- **Overall site layout** (95–103): building complex as clusters of smaller buildings rather than one monolith, story-count limits, shielded/screened parking, circulation realms, pedestrian streets, family-of-entrances grouping, small distributed parking lots instead of one large lot.
- **Positioning buildings on site** (104–109, partly detailed above): site repair, south-facing orientation for solar access, positive outdoor space, "wings of light" (buildings shaped as thin wings so every room can reach exterior light), connected buildings, long thin house massing.
- **Courtyards, roofs, hierarchy of open space** (113–118): car/parking connections, hierarchy of open space (every space wants both a smaller space "backing" it and a larger space it opens onto), courtyards that stay lively rather than becoming dead gravel voids, cascading/stepped roof profiles, deep sheltering roof overhangs, roof gardens.
- **Rooms and social gradients within a building, cont'd** (128, 130): indoor sunlight matched to the intimacy gradient's most-used rooms, entrance room as the most formal/public interior space.
- **Named rooms for houses and workplaces** (136–158): couple's and children's realms, sleeping oriented east, farmhouse kitchen as social hub, private street-facing terraces, a private "room of one's own," sequences of sitting spaces at different intimacy levels, bed clusters/alcoves, bathing rooms, bulk storage; flexible office space, communal eating, small work groups, staged reception sequences, waiting areas, small meeting rooms, half-private offices; secondary structures (rentable rooms, teenager's cottage, home workshops, open exterior stairs).
- **Inside/outside edge treatment** (159–168, partly detailed above): light on two sides of every room, building edge, sunny vs. north-facing zoning, outdoor rooms, opening walls to the street, gallery surrounds, balconies, physical connection to the ground.
- **Gardens** (169–178): terraced slopes, fruit trees, tree places (detailed above), gardens deliberately left to grow wild, garden walls, trellised walks, greenhouses, garden seating, kitchen gardens, compost.
- **Alcoves and minor rooms** (179–189): alcoves generally, window place (detailed above), the fireplace as an emotional anchor citing Bachelard's writing on fire as a near-primal need, eating nooks, workspace enclosures, kitchen layout, sitting circle (detailed above), communal and separate sleeping arrangements, marriage bed, dressing rooms.
- **Room fine-tuning** (190–204): varied ceiling heights, indoor space shaped as convex/positive volume (the interior mirror of Positive Outdoor Space), half-open walls between spaces, interior windows, staircase volume, corner-placed doors, thick walls that create usable depth for shelves/alcoves/seats, built-in seating, child-scaled hideaway spaces, a "secret place."

## Construction and Ornament (Patterns 205–253)

The final section moves to buildable structural and finishing detail — largely below the scale relevant to level layout, but a few ideas are worth carrying over as texture/ambience principles rather than structural ones.

- **Structural philosophy and layout** (205–220): letting structure follow the social plan rather than imposing a grid on it, "gradual stiffening" (moving from rough scheme to precise structure incrementally rather than all at once), root/foundation systems, wall and vault construction.
- **Openings** (221–232): natural, deep-set door and window openings, low sills and deep reveals (cited repeatedly by earlier patterns to soften light and deepen the sense of enclosure at a window), deliberately low doorways, subsidiary details like column "places" thick enough to lean against.
- **Surfaces and indoor detail** (233–240): floor surfaces, layered exterior walls, soft/warm interior wall materials, wide-opening windows, glazed solid doors, filtered/diffused light, small window panes, fine trim.
- **Outdoor finishing** (241–248, partly detailed above via Seat Spots): front-door benches, sitting walls, canvas/fabric roofing, raised flower beds, climbing plants, cracked/irregular paving, soft brick and tile.
- **Ornament, color, and personalization** (249–253): decorative ornament as meaningful pattern rather than applied decoration, warm color palettes, deliberately mismatched/varied furniture ("different chairs" as a value, not a flaw), pools of light (detailed above), and a closing pattern arguing that a finished space should be filled with the actual objects of an occupant's life rather than generic "interior design" — the book explicitly frames sterile, magazine-styled decor as worse than a room cluttered with a person's real, meaningful possessions.
