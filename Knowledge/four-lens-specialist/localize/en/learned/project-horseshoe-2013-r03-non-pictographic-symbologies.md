# Exploring Non-pictographic Symbologies for Games — Learned Notes

## Symbols as High-Density Representation

The report contrasts an expensive, detailed dragon model with a compact glyph capable of showing faction, threat, health, status, and role at a glance. Pictorial fidelity and informational fidelity are different goals. Symbolic presentation can make complex strategy readable and reduce asset cost, while inviting players to imagine more than the display literally shows.

The tradeoff is learning. A glyph language becomes powerful only after players internalize consistent mappings. Onboarding, progressive introduction, reference tools, and stable grammar are part of the system—not optional documentation.

## Taxonomy Before Glyphs

The proposed workflow is:

1. List information players can know.
2. Identify decisions each item supports.
3. Group attributes into a data taxonomy.
4. Rank categories by urgency and frequency.
5. Map categories onto perceptually appropriate channels.
6. Generate glyphs and test recognition in realistic scenes.

This prevents an icon from accumulating unrelated meanings and reveals when the interface exposes data that never affects a decision.

## Visual Channel Hierarchy

Pre-attentive perception detects some differences faster than others. Color is especially strong, while size, shape, and orientation also support rapid distinction. Texture and precise position usually require more attention. Containment, spatial relationship, motion, and temporal change can communicate structure or status beyond a static mark.

Mappings depend on context. Color cannot be the only carrier of critical state; changing size may break a fixed grid; animation can distract or create sensory burden. Pair channels for critical information, respect cultural associations, and test under color-vision variation, small displays, motion reduction, clutter, and time pressure.

### Glyph Test Metrics

- Recognition time
- Confusion pairs
- Error under clutter
- Recall after absence
- Performance without color
- Learnability of new combinations
- Decision accuracy, not merely symbol naming
