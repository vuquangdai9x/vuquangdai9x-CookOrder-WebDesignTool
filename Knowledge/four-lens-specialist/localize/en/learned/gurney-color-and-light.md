# Color and Light: A Guide for the Realist Painter — Learned Notes

Note on source: this PDF is almost entirely full-page illustrations and demonstration
paintings; the only machine-extractable text is the front matter and a set of short
per-topic blurbs (roughly one paragraph per named section). The notes below expand
those topic blurbs into concrete, practical guidance in original wording, organized
under the book's real chapter/section structure. Anywhere a concept needed more
context than the blurb gave, it is filled in from well-established, non-proprietary
color-and-light theory that the book is itself teaching (value scales, warm/cool
relationships, atmospheric perspective, etc.), not from copying book prose.

## Chapter: History of Light and Color in Painting

Gurney opens by grounding technique in art history, arguing that painters throughout
history worked under real constraints (limited pigments, no photography, changing
taste) that shaped how they used light and color — and that understanding those
constraints makes their solutions more transferable, not less.

### Old Masters' Color
Historical painters (Vermeer is the example used) worked with a strikingly small
palette — often well under twenty pigments — yet achieved rich, convincing color.
The lesson is that a disciplined, limited palette forces more thoughtful value and
temperature relationships than an unlimited one; scarcity of hues was a feature, not
a limitation, of pre-modern painting.

### The Academic Tradition
Formal academic training emphasized structured value drawing and controlled studio
lighting before color, building a rigorous foundation of tonal accuracy that later
movements reacted against or built upon.

### The Hudson River School
These landscape painters used light as an emotional and compositional device —
light appears to originate from within the scene rather than simply illuminating it
from outside. Large studio compositions were synthesized from smaller plein-air
studies, combining observed accuracy with staged, idealized illumination.

### Plein-Air Movements
Painting on location, directly from the observed subject, prioritizes capturing
transient, real light conditions over studio invention. This tradition is the root
of most of the book's observational color practices.

### Symbolist Dreams
Some painters used light and color non-literally, to evoke mood and psychological
states rather than to describe a scene accurately — a reminder that lighting choices
are also a narrative/emotional tool, not only a physical-accuracy problem.

### Magazine Illustration
Commercial illustration developed its own fast, high-contrast, attention-grabbing
approach to light and color, optimized for reproduction and quick reading rather
than gallery viewing — an ancestor of the graphic, readable lighting used in games
and concept art today.

## Chapter: Sources of Light

The physical character of a light source (its direction, hardness, and color)
determines almost everything about how a scene reads. Gurney treats each common
real-world light source as having a distinct signature worth memorizing.

### Direct Sunlight
A clear sunny scene actually contains three overlapping light systems: the sun
itself (a small, hard, warm source), the blue sky (a huge, soft, cool fill light),
and light reflected off illuminated surfaces (bounce light, always a subordinate,
weaker source). Treat the sun as the dominant system and the other two as always
secondary to it in both intensity and color influence.

### Overcast Light
An overcast sky acts as a giant diffuser: shadows soften or disappear, contrast
between light and shadow value drops dramatically, and color becomes more uniform
across a form. Overcast light is easier to read as "soft/flat" lighting when
matching a mood or avoiding harsh cast shadows.

### Window Light
Light entering a room through a window is directional but softened, and stays
consistent over time (unlike direct sun, which moves). It naturally simplifies a
subject's value pattern because it comes from one general area, making it a
reliable, popular choice for interior and portrait lighting setups.

### Indoor Electric Light
Artificial light sources vary along three practical axes worth evaluating for any
light: relative brightness (how strong compared to other sources in the scene),
hardness/softness (point source vs. diffused fixture), and color (incandescent
skews warm/orange, fluorescent historically skews green/cool). Mixed indoor
lighting scenes often combine two or more of these with different colors, which is
what makes them visually complex.

### Streetlights and Night Conditions
Night scenes historically had only two light colors available: cool blue-gray
moonlight and warm orange firelight. Modern electric street lighting added new hues
(sodium-vapor orange, mercury-vapor blue-green, LED white), so a believable night
scene should pick a limited, deliberate palette of 2–3 light colors rather than
letting every light source be arbitrary.

### Luminescence
Distinct from incandescence (glowing from heat), luminescence is light given off at
cool temperatures — bioluminescence, phosphorescence, chemical glows. It reads
differently from a hot light source: often even, saturated, and without the
warm-to-white heat gradient of fire or filament light, useful for stylized/fantasy
light sources (glowing crystals, magic effects) in game art.

## Chapter: Light and Form

This chapter deals with how light reveals three-dimensional form on a surface,
independent of light source.

### Separation of Light and Shadow
In direct light, the lit side and shadow side of a form can differ by several full
steps on a value scale (the book gives up to about five steps in strong sunlight).
Keeping that light/shadow value gap consistent across every form in a scene — the
way a musician keeps a consistent musical interval — is what makes a whole painting
(or lit 3D scene) feel unified rather than arbitrary.

### Occlusion Shadows
The darkest, most concentrated shadow accents in a scene occur where two surfaces
crowd close together and block ambient light from reaching the crevice — fabric
folds, where an object touches the ground, tight interior corners. These small dark
accents (contact shadows / ambient occlusion, in 3D-rendering terms) are what
anchors objects visually to their surroundings and to the ground plane.

### Frontal Lighting
Lighting a subject from near the viewer's position minimizes visible shadow and
flattens perceived form, since shadows fall mostly behind the object, out of view —
useful for even, legible readability but weak for conveying volume.

### Spotlighting
Theatrical/selective lighting is deliberately uneven: important areas are lit
brightly while everything else is allowed to fall into shadow, directing the
viewer's attention exactly where the artist wants it. This is a compositional tool
as much as a physical one — controlling value contrast to control what the eye
reads first.

## Chapter: Elements of Color

Core vocabulary and observational habits for working with color, independent of any
specific light source.

### Chroma and Value
When painting from life, the visible range of light-to-dark and dull-to-saturated
in the real world usually exceeds what any pigment or screen can reproduce, so the
artist must compress and choose which relationships to preserve. Value (light/dark)
relationships generally matter more for legibility than exact hue matching.

### Local Color
"Local color" is the inherent color of a surface as it would appear in neutral
white light, isolated from its surroundings. The color that same surface actually
displays in a given scene is usually shifted from its local color by the light
source's color and by reflected light from nearby objects — local color is a useful
reference point, not the color to actually paint.

### Grays and Neutrals
Desaturated, neutral colors are not a failure state — they are essential contrast
partners that make the scene's few areas of true saturation read as vivid. Most
color mixing failures come from overusing intense color everywhere, not from using
too much gray.

### The Green Problem
Green is highlighted as a particularly hard color to mix and control convincingly
(foliage especially), because it's easy to oversaturate or make it read as flat and
artificial; deliberately varying green's value, temperature, and chroma across a
scene (rather than one uniform green) keeps foliage and landscape believable.

### Gradation
A smooth transition between two color states — hue to hue, light to dark, or dull
to saturated — reads the way a musical glissando sounds: continuous rather than
stepped. Gradation is what keeps large forms (skies, ground planes, skin) from
looking flat or banded.

### Tints
Adding white to a color produces a tint (a lightened, often "pastel" version of the
hue). Tints are the natural color state of distant, hazy, or strongly lit objects,
and are typically the right choice for large-scale/mural-style work that needs to
read cleanly from a distance.

### The Search for Pigments
Historically, useful pigments had to satisfy three practical constraints:
permanence (doesn't fade), availability, and safety (nonpoisonous) — a reminder
that real-world material constraints shaped the historical color vocabulary artists
had to work within, distinct from the theoretically unlimited color space available
digitally today.

## Chapter: Paint and Pigments

Practical studio-craft chapter on organizing and mixing physical paint — most
directly relevant to traditional/2D concept art workflows, but the underlying
discipline (limited, deliberate palettes; planned value ranges) transfers directly
to digital and real-time color grading.

### Charting Pigments / Palette Arrangements
Systematically testing and arranging available pigments (e.g., by hue, temperature,
or mixing behavior) before painting builds a working knowledge of what a limited
palette can and can't reach, avoiding time wasted mixing blindly mid-painting.

### Warm Underpainting
Starting a painting with a warm-toned base layer is a traditional technique that
lets warm color show through in later, thinner layers, unifying the overall
temperature of a piece and giving cool passages more visual "pop" by contrast.

### The Mud Debate
There are two competing schools of thought on whether "muddy" (over-mixed, low
chroma, indeterminate) color is always a mistake, or is sometimes exactly the right
neutral note a passage needs — the practical takeaway is that "muddiness" is
often really a mismatch between an area's chroma and its intended value/role in the
scene, not an absolute flaw.

### Monochromatic Schemes
A color scheme built from a single hue carried through a full range of value and
chroma (e.g., all warm brown-grays) has a long tradition and produces strong
unity almost automatically — a useful fallback scheme for value-first compositional
studies (lighting/thumbnail passes) before color is finalized.

### Warm and Cool
Every color can be judged not just by hue/value/chroma but by relative temperature,
and warm/cool relationships between adjacent colors are often more important to
convincing lighting than exact hue accuracy — e.g., a warm light source implies
cool shadows (and vice versa) as a default, reliable relationship.

### Mixing Color Strings
A "color string" is a pre-mixed set of paint blobs of one hue stepped from light to
dark value, prepared before painting begins. Premixing strings for the scene's key
colors saves time during observational/timed painting and forces the artist to
commit to a value plan in advance — directly analogous to building a limited
in-engine color/material palette or a lighting LUT before final lighting pass.

## Chapter: Gamut Mapping

Gamut mapping is Gurney's system for deliberately restricting and controlling a
painting's color palette using the color wheel as a working tool, rather than
mixing colors ad hoc.

### Creating Gamut Masks
A color scheme can be described as a shape (polygon) drawn on top of a color wheel,
enclosing only the hues that scheme uses. A physical or digital mask cut to that
shape can be moved around the wheel to generate new, related schemes quickly —
conceptually identical to rotating a limited swatch/LUT palette around a hue wheel
to generate palette variants for different times of day or moods.

### Shapes of Color Schemes
Gamut shapes vary: a scheme might be a tight cluster (near-monochromatic), a
triangle or diamond spanning a few related hues, or a wide arc spanning much of the
wheel. Narrower gamut shapes produce more unified, harmonious results; wider shapes
allow more contrast and drama but risk incoherence if not value-controlled.

### Mixing a Controlled Gamut
Once a gamut shape is chosen, every color used in the piece is mixed from within
that shape's boundary, guaranteeing the whole piece stays color-harmonious by
construction rather than by after-the-fact correction.

### Color Scripting
Planning a piece's (or a whole production's) color and value progression in advance
— effectively a color/value storyboard — the same practice studios use for a film
or game's "color script" to ensure levels/scenes read distinctly and support the
narrative's emotional arc scene to scene.

## Chapter: Visual Perception

How human color/light perception actually works, and where it diverges from
physical reality — important because a painter (or a game's lighting artist) is
designing for perception, not for physical accuracy alone.

### A World without Color
Perception of color is a construction of the visual system, not a direct read of
physical wavelength — the same physical light can be perceived very differently
depending on context, adaptation, and surrounding colors.

### Is Moonlight Blue?
A commonly cited example: moonlight is physically closer to reddish in wavelength
composition, but is almost universally perceived and painted as blue, because human
night vision (rod-dominant, low-color-discrimination) combined with learned
convention makes "moonlight = blue" the correct perceptual/communicative choice
even though it isn't the physically literal one. Painting (and game lighting) should
target perceived correctness over literal physical correctness when the two
conflict.

### Adaptation and Contrast
The perceived color of any area is influenced by the colors around it (simultaneous
contrast) and by recent visual exposure (chromatic adaptation) — both in flat 2D
arrangements and in full 3D scenes. A color swatch or in-game surface can look
completely different depending on what surrounds it, which is why color/lighting
should always be evaluated in context, never in isolation.

### Appetizing and Healing Colors
Certain colors and color groupings are associated with physiological/psychological
responses (appetite stimulation, calm, alertness) — a reminder that color choices in
environment and UI design carry associative/emotional weight beyond pure
readability, and should be chosen deliberately for spaces where mood matters (a
tavern vs. a hospital vs. a horror corridor).

## Chapter: Surfaces and Effects

Covers how light interacts with different materials and atmospheric conditions —
the most directly transferable chapter for environment and material shading work.

### Transmitted Light
Light passing through a translucent or transparent material (glass, leaves, thin
fabric) behaves differently from light bouncing off an opaque surface — it picks up
and carries the material's color, and produces characteristic glows and color casts
worth treating as a distinct lighting case from reflection.

### Subsurface Scattering
Light entering a translucent material (skin, wax, milk, fruit, foliage) spreads out
beneath the surface before re-emerging, producing a soft internal glow rather than a
hard reflected highlight — most visible at thin, back-lit edges (an ear, fingers,
a leaf). This is the traditional-painting description of the same physical effect
modern render engines model explicitly as SSS.

### Specular Reflections
Shiny surfaces act like mirrors, reflecting an image of their surroundings rather
than showing their "own" local color — a car hood reflects tree branches, a chrome
hubcap reflects sky and road. Painting (or shading) a specular surface means
painting the environment reflected in it, not the object's material color.

### Sky Blue
A daytime sky is not a flat color; it's the overlap of two independent gradients —
"solar glare" (brightness/desaturation increasing toward the sun) and "horizon
glow" (color shifting, usually paling, toward the horizon regardless of sun
position). Treating sky color as these two overlapping gradients rather than a
single flat or single-gradient blue produces far more convincing skies.

### Atmospheric Perspective
As distance increases, intervening atmosphere lightens, desaturates, and cools (or
warms, depending on conditions) the color of distant objects, and reduces contrast
— the standard "warm/sharp advances, cool/soft recedes" depth cue. This is one of
the most directly game-relevant principles: distance fog, aerial-perspective LUTs,
and background desaturation in engine rendering are direct implementations of this
same observed effect.

### Reverse Atmospheric Perspective
Under specific rare conditions (certain sunset/backlit/hazy conditions), the usual
rule flips and distant elements actually read warmer than the foreground. Useful as
a deliberate stylistic choice for a striking or unusual sky/vista moment, as long as
it's a conscious exception rather than an accidental contradiction of the normal
depth cue.

### Sunsets / Fog, Mist, Smoke, Dust / Skyholes and Foliage / Sunbeams and Shadowbeams
A family of specific atmospheric-effect studies: sunsets push the whole scene's
color temperature and value range to extremes near the horizon; fog/mist/smoke/dust
each scatter and diffuse light differently (density and particle size change how
hard shadows and edges remain visible through them); "skyholes" are gaps in foliage
or cloud that let bright sky value punch through a darker mass, useful compositional
accents; sunbeams and their shadowbeam counterparts (visible light shafts, and the
dark bands cast by objects blocking them) render volumetric light passing through
a participating medium — directly analogous to volumetric/god-ray lighting in game
engines.

### Illuminated Foreground
Contrary to the traditional convention of keeping the foreground in shadow (common
in classical European/American landscape composition) and the middle distance lit,
an alternative approach lights the immediate foreground with full detail and pushes
the middle distance into shadow instead — a compositional choice that changes where
the eye enters and travels through a scene.

### Mountain Streams
Fast, turbulent water (as opposed to still lowland water) behaves differently
optically: it's typically clearer, and its motion breaks reflections into rapids,
ripples, eddies, and standing-wave "haystacks," each of which catches and scatters
light differently than a calm reflective surface.

## Chapter: Light's Changing Show / Serial Painting

Closes the technique portion of the book by looking at light as something that
changes over time rather than a fixed condition to capture once: painting (or
designing) the same subject under a series of different times of day, seasons, or
weather conditions builds a much deeper, more flexible understanding of how a given
form and material actually behave under light than a single rendering ever can —
directly analogous to building a full day/night or seasonal lighting cycle for a
game environment rather than lighting only for one "hero" screenshot moment.

## Key Takeaways for Environment and Lighting Art

- Every believable lighting setup is really 2–3 distinct light sources layered
  together (key/sun, sky/fill, bounce), each subordinate in intensity and color
  dominance to the one above it — use this as a checklist when a scene's lighting
  reads as flat or arbitrary. → § Chapter: Sources of Light
- Keep the light-side/shadow-side value gap consistent across every surface in a
  scene (the "musical interval" idea) so the whole piece reads as one coherent
  lighting condition rather than each object being lit independently. → § Chapter: Light and Form
- Treat night, interior, and mixed-source scenes as a deliberate 2–3 color light
  palette (not arbitrary per-light hues), the way historical night scenes were
  built from only moonlight-blue and firelight-orange. → § Chapter: Sources of Light
- Use occlusion/contact shadows deliberately at every point where forms meet or
  crowd together — they're what visually anchors objects to the ground and to each
  other, equivalent to ambient occlusion in a render pipeline. → § Chapter: Light and Form
- Build atmospheric depth (fog, aerial perspective, distance desaturation/cooling)
  as a first-class depth cue, not just a technical fog volume — it is one of the
  most reliable ways to sell scale and distance in an environment. → § Chapter: Surfaces and Effects
- For skies, model two overlapping gradients (glare toward the sun, glow toward the
  horizon) rather than one flat gradient, for far more convincing sky shaders/gradients. → § Chapter: Surfaces and Effects
- Favor perceptual correctness over literal physical correctness when they conflict
  (the "moonlight is blue" case) — players will read a scene as wrong if it's
  physically accurate but perceptually unfamiliar. → § Chapter: Visual Perception
- Use gamut mapping (a restricted, wheel-based hue range per scene/level) to
  guarantee color harmony by construction, and treat a "color script" pass —
  planning value/color across a whole sequence of scenes — as essential for
  environment art direction, not just film production. → § Chapter: Gamut Mapping
- Reserve saturated color as a scarce resource against a mostly neutral/gray
  backdrop; overusing intense color everywhere is a more common failure than being
  "too gray." → § Chapter: Elements of Color
- Light and lighting choices carry emotional/associative meaning (appetite,
  comfort, dread) independent of physical accuracy — choose environment palettes
  deliberately for the mood a space needs to convey. → § Chapter: Visual Perception
- Design key materials (skin, foliage, glass, water, metal) around their distinct
  optical behavior — subsurface scattering, transmission, specular reflection of
  environment, turbulent-water light breakup — rather than a single generic shading
  model for everything. → § Chapter: Surfaces and Effects
- Light a subject/environment under several different conditions (time of day,
  weather) rather than only for one hero shot, to build a lighting rig flexible
  enough to hold up across a full gameplay session. → § Chapter: Light's Changing Show / Serial Painting
