## Chapter: Don't make me think!

Krug's First Law of Usability: every screen should be self-evident — obvious enough that a user "gets it" (what it is, what it does) without expending conscious thought. Where full self-evidence isn't possible (novel or complex features), the fallback bar is self-explanatory: it takes a little thought, but only a little. Sources of unnecessary "thinking" include cute/internal/marketing-driven naming for things that need plain labels, and controls whose clickability (or interactivity) isn't visually obvious. Krug frames every moment of user hesitation as a "question mark" over the user's head; the designer's job is to eliminate question marks, because each one adds cognitive load and erodes confidence in the product, even when the user eventually muddles through successfully. The deeper reason this matters isn't fickleness or competition — it's that people spend far less focused attention on any given screen than designers assume, so screens must do their explanatory work almost entirely at a glance.

**Game UX bridge**: Menu items named after in-fiction lore terms ("the Sanctum," "Aetherforge") instead of their real function ("Crafting," "Skill Tree") force the exact kind of unnecessary thinking Krug warns about — cute/internal naming beating plain description. HUD elements that look decorative rather than interactive (a glowing icon that's actually a clickable button, with no affordance cue) create the same "is this clickable?" hesitation Krug describes for Web links. A new player's first 10-30 seconds in a menu screen is the game's version of the 50-millisecond first impression: if the top-level navigation isn't self-evident, players start forming wrong mental models that compound with every subsequent screen.

## Chapter: How we really use the Web

Three "facts of life" about real user behavior, offered as a corrective to the designer's fantasy of an attentive, deliberate reader: (1) Users scan rather than read, because they're usually on a task-focused mission, already know most of a page is irrelevant to them, and are practiced scanners from a lifetime of print and digital media. (2) Users satisfice rather than optimize — per Herbert Simon's term and Gary Klein's naturalistic-decision-making research on time-pressured experts (firefighters, pilots), people take the first reasonable option rather than comparing all options, because weighing alternatives is slow and the cost of a wrong guess is usually just a click of the Back button. (3) Users muddle through rather than build an accurate mental model of how something works — most people operate technology successfully using vague, sometimes-wrong theories about its internals, because understanding the mechanism isn't important to them; they only care that the thing works, and once something works they stop looking for a better way.

**Game UX bridge**: Players scan HUDs and menus the same way — they're not reading tooltip paragraphs, they're pattern-matching icon shapes and colors against a target. Satisficing explains why players click the first plausible menu entry rather than comparing every submenu (which argues for making the "right" first click nearly always available, rather than punishing wrong guesses with long undo paths). Muddling-through explains why players often succeed at core loops using entirely wrong mental models of underlying systems (e.g., believing "block" reduces damage by a flat amount when it's actually a percentage) — which is fine until the wrong model breaks down at a harder difficulty, so critical systems (stamina, aggro, damage formulas) benefit from being made self-evident rather than left to be reverse-engineered.

## Chapter: Billboard Design 101

Because users are "billboards going by at 60 miles an hour," not readers, screens should be designed for scanning. Concrete techniques: (1) Follow existing conventions (standardized placement/appearance of things like logos, navigation, and shopping carts) so users don't have to relearn basics on every new page — but apply the rule "clarity trumps consistency" when breaking a convention makes something meaningfully clearer. (2) Build a clear visual hierarchy: more-important elements are more visually prominent (size, boldness, color, whitespace, position); logically related items look visually related (grouping, shared style); nested relationships (section contains subsection) are shown by visual containment. A flawed hierarchy is like a garbled sentence — technically parseable, but it forces re-reading. (3) Break the page into clearly defined regions so users can decide at a glance which regions to focus on and which to ignore (the extreme case being "banner blindness," where users learn to not even see ad-shaped regions). (4) Make clickability obvious through consistent visual cues (shape, location, color/underline) rather than relying on interaction (hover) to reveal it. (5) Minimize visual noise — Krug identifies three kinds: "shouting" (everything competing for attention because no priority was chosen), disorganization (no underlying grid), and clutter (too much low-value content diluting the useful content's signal). (6) Format text for scanning: generous, well-written headings positioned close to the content they introduce (not "floating" ambiguously between sections), short paragraphs, bulleted lists wherever items are naturally enumerable, and selective bolding of key terms.

**Game UX bridge**: Conventions matter enormously in games — players expect the pause menu in a predictable place, inventory grids to behave like every other inventory grid they've used, and health to be top-left or bottom-center depending on genre convention; deviating without a strong reason costs onboarding time industry-wide. Visual hierarchy is the entire logic of good HUD design: the target reticle, ammo count, and health bar need to be instantly separable from decorative environment art, and different HUD priority levels (critical alerts vs. ambient flavor text) need genuinely different visual weight, not just different positions. Banner blindness has a direct analogue in "notification blindness" — if cosmetic-shop badges and real system alerts share the same visual treatment, players learn to ignore both. The noise categories map directly onto HUD bloat: too many always-on icons (shouting), inconsistent grid alignment across menu screens (disorganization), and low-value system messages crowding out combat feedback (clutter).

## Chapter: Animal, Vegetable, or Mineral?

Krug's Second Law of Usability: users don't mind many clicks/taps as long as each one is a mindless, low-uncertainty choice — a rough rule of thumb is that three unambiguous clicks feel equivalent to one click requiring real thought. Ambiguous top-level choices (where the user can't confidently tell which of two labeled paths applies to their situation) create hesitation and erode confidence even before the destination page loads. Where a genuinely hard choice can't be avoided (forms are the classic case), guidance should be brief, timely (encountered exactly when needed), and unavoidable (formatted so it can't be missed) — the London "LOOK RIGHT" street-corner example is held up as the ideal: minimal, exactly-timed, impossible to miss.

**Game UX bridge**: This directly informs difficulty-select screens, class/build pickers, and branching dialogue menus — the number of choices matters less than whether each choice is confidently disambiguated. A crafting menu that forces the player to guess between two similarly worded recipe categories creates exactly the "which mailbox?" hesitation Krug describes. Just-in-time contextual tooltips (appearing only when a new mechanic is first encountered, not front-loaded into a wall of tutorial text) are the game-UX equivalent of Krug's brief/timely/unavoidable guidance standard.

## Chapter: Omit needless words

Krug's Third Law: cut roughly half the words on a screen, then cut half of what's left — an intentionally aggressive framing meant to counter designers' instinct to over-explain. Excess text raises noise, buries the content that matters, and makes pages feel more effortful even if no one reads the extra words. Two categories are singled out as near-worthless: "happy talk" (self-congratulatory welcome text that conveys no actionable information, common on home pages and section fronts) and unnecessary instructions (which most users skip entirely until muddling-through has already failed, and which should be replaced by making the interface self-explanatory rather than explaining it in prose).

**Game UX bridge**: Loading-screen lore dumps, quest-log walls of text, and tutorial pop-ups that front-load every mechanic before the player has touched the controller are the game-industry equivalent of happy talk and over-instruction. The fix Krug recommends — cut ruthlessly, and replace explanation with self-explanatory design — argues for teaching mechanics through minimal, contextual prompts triggered at first use (à la a "LOOK RIGHT" sign) rather than expository tutorial text the player will skip anyway.

## Chapter: Street signs and Breadcrumbs

Navigation design chapter, using the mall/department-store analogy: shoppers use signage plus browsing behavior to find things, deciding early on whether to ask for help (search) or browse (navigate hierarchy) based on trust, familiarity, and urgency. Web navigation has to compensate for missing physical cues that real-world navigation relies on — no sense of scale (how big is this site?), no sense of direction, and no accumulated spatial memory (clicking "teleports" rather than traverses, so users can't build a mental map the way they would in a store). This is why Krug argues navigation *is* the site, not just a feature of it: it reveals what content exists, implicitly teaches how to use the site, and continuously signals competence ("do these people know what they're doing?").

Persistent (global) navigation should appear on every page except focused task flows like checkout forms, and should include four elements: Site ID (top-left, framing the page, acting as a Home link), Sections (primary navigation, top-level hierarchy), Utilities (account/help/search-type links, limited to 4-5 most-used items with the rest pushed to a footer), and a way to search (a literal box+button, labeled plainly "Search," with no unnecessary options or instructional copy cluttering it). Lower-level (third-level-and-deeper) navigation is repeatedly under-designed across the industry, because it gets the least design attention despite carrying real traffic — Krug's advice is to design sample pages for all levels of the hierarchy before debating visual style.

Page names function as street signs: every page needs a visible, prominent name that matches (or closely approximates) the label the user clicked to get there — a broken "name doesn't match what I clicked" contract forces users to consciously double-check they're in the right place. "You are here" indicators and breadcrumbs both address a site's inherent "lost in space" feeling; breadcrumbs work best placed at the top, using ">" separators, with the current page bolded and non-clickable. Krug singles out tabs as an underused, near-ideal navigation pattern because they're self-evident, hard to miss, and visually satisfying when built correctly (the active tab must visually "pop" forward).

The "trunk test" is offered as a portable acid test: dropped blindfolded onto a random deep page, can the user immediately answer — what site is this, what page am I on, what are the main sections, what are my options here, where am I in the hierarchy, and how do I search? — using only a quick, slightly-blurred glance, not close study.

**Game UX bridge**: The trunk test maps almost perfectly onto game menu design — drop a player into any random sub-menu (deep in a settings tree, three tabs into inventory) and ask whether they can instantly answer "what screen is this," "what are my siblings/parent options," and "how do I get back/home." Persistent navigation's four elements have direct game analogues: a stable menu title/icon (Site ID), consistently placed top-level tabs (Sections), always-available account/settings/help icons (Utilities), and — in content-heavy games — an in-game search or filter for inventories/codexes/crafting lists. The "teleportation with no traversal" problem is arguably worse in games than on the Web, since menu-to-menu jumps (inventory → crafting → map) offer zero spatial continuity cues; breadcrumb-style trails or a persistent "you are here" tab highlight substitute for the missing spatial memory. Under-designed deep navigation is a common failure mode in games too: the first two menu tiers (main menu, pause menu) get lavish art passes while tier-three-and-below screens (a specific settings sub-panel, a rare crafting sub-category) are visually inconsistent afterthoughts.

## Chapter: The Big Bang Theory of Web Design

The Home page must simultaneously satisfy many concrete needs (identity/mission, hierarchy overview, search, "teases" of good content, timely/fresh content signaling the site is alive, promotional deals, shortcuts to top requests, registration/sign-in) and abstract goals (show me what I'm looking for and what I'm not, show me where to start, establish trust) — all under fierce internal competition for the limited, high-value "above the fold" space (the "tragedy of the commons" dynamic, where every stakeholder wants a promoted link because it works too well, degrading the page for everyone). The single most common failure Krug observes in home-page reviews is that the big picture — what is this, concretely — never gets stated clearly enough, because unlike every other stakeholder need, "explain what this is" has no natural internal advocate. Krug's research citation ("50 milliseconds to make a good first impression") supports treating first-glance impressions as reliable predictors of later, more considered judgments — and warns that if a user's first guess about what something is turns out wrong, they misinterpret everything that follows and get progressively "loster," not less lost, over time. Even in an era where users increasingly land deep in a site via a direct link rather than the home page, they still frequently "surface" to the home page afterward to get their bearings — so the orienting job the home page does is still necessary, just relocated in the user's journey. Concrete techniques for conveying the big picture: a tagline (6-8 words, specific enough that no competitor could use it, distinct from a vaguer "motto"), a short welcome blurb (not a corporate mission statement — nobody reads those), and, increasingly, a short explainer video.

**Game UX bridge**: The main menu and first-launch screen carry the same "Big Bang" burden — identity, top-level options, "what can I do here," and trust-building all compete for one screen's worth of space, and playtesters (like Krug's usability-test subjects) reliably reveal that the most basic orienting information ("what mode am I even in — story, multiplayer, sandbox?") got assumed rather than stated. The tragedy-of-the-commons dynamic shows up as feature creep on title/hub screens — every system (battle pass, daily login, store, guild) wants main-menu real estate, and each addition individually "works" (drives engagement) while collectively degrading the screen's clarity. First-impression research supports front-loading a short, skippable "what is this game/mode" explainer at first launch rather than relying on players to infer it from menu browsing alone, especially for genre-blending or unconventional titles.

## Chapter: "The Farmer and the Cowman Should Be Friends"

Krug diagnoses why usability arguments on dev teams rarely resolve: individuals project their personal likes/dislikes onto "most users" (the fallacy that other people are basically like us), and different roles carry different professional biases — designers optimize for visual pleasure, developers for interesting complexity/craft, and "hype culture" stakeholders (marketing, leadership) for promises and differentiation, creating a structural tension with "craft culture" (design/dev) who have to deliver on those promises. The deeper myth underlying all of this is belief in an "Average User" with consistent, describable preferences; Krug's position, from extensive observation, is that all user behavior is fundamentally idiosyncratic, and debates framed as "do people like X" are close to unanswerable and unproductive. His antidote is reframing the question from "is X good" (a matter of opinion) to "does this specific X, worded this way, in this context, work for people trying to do this task" (a testable question) — and settling it through usability testing rather than argument, which also has the side benefit of breaking teams out of the Average User fallacy by exposing them directly to how varied real users' reactions actually are.

**Game UX bridge**: The same role-based tension plays out on game teams — artists optimizing HUD for visual minimalism vs. systems designers wanting every stat visible, narrative wanting more in-fiction naming vs. UX wanting plain labels. "Do players like radial menus" is exactly the unanswerable framing Krug warns against; "does this radial menu, with these 8 items, let players pick their intended item under combat time pressure" is the testable reframe. Playtesting a specific menu/HUD prototype with a handful of real players settles far more design arguments than another round of internal opinion, and — as with Web teams — exposes designers to how differently non-designers actually parse a screen they've stopped seeing freshly themselves.

## Chapter: Usability testing on 10 cents a day

Krug's central pitch is that usability testing should be cheap, frequent, and small-scale rather than rare and expensive — three participants tested for a morning, once a month (or every couple weeks under Agile), consistently surfaces enough high-value problems to keep a team busy fixing things until the next round. He's emphatic that focus groups (group opinion/discussion) are not a substitute for usability tests (individual, observed, task-based, think-aloud) — focus groups answer "what do people want/like," usability tests answer "can people actually use this." Key operating principles: testing one user is meaningfully better than testing none; testing early with one user beats testing late with fifty, because early findings are still cheap to act on; you can never find every problem in one round (and shouldn't try to), because "you can find more problems in half a day than you can fix in a month," so the goal is a steady cadence, not exhaustive coverage. Recruiting advice: "recruit loosely and grade on a curve" — precise target-demographic matching costs more effort than it's worth for most sites, and testing with some participants *outside* the target audience is valuable because domain experts are rarely bothered by clarity aimed at beginners, while genuine novices reveal problems experts have stopped noticing. The facilitator's core job is getting participants to think aloud and never leading them (no hints beyond "what would you do if I weren't here"), and observation by as many team members/stakeholders as possible is treated as high-value in itself, because watching a real user struggle is far more persuasive than a report describing it. Post-test, the debrief prioritizes a short, collectively-voted list of the worst observed problems ("focus ruthlessly on fixing the most serious problems first") over an exhaustive fix-everything backlog, explicitly ignoring both self-correcting "kayak" problems (momentary confusion the user recovers from unaided) and unsolicited feature requests (which participants can rarely justify once asked to elaborate).

**Game UX bridge**: The "morning a month, three players" cadence is directly portable to playtesting menu/UI/onboarding flows during production — cheap enough that it should happen continuously rather than only pre-launch. The focus-group-vs-usability-test distinction maps onto the common mistake of trusting community sentiment/surveys ("do players want X feature") to answer interface questions that only observed play can answer ("can players find X feature when they need it"). "Recruit loosely, grade on a curve" argues against over-indexing tests on veteran players/streamers who no longer represent a first-time player's confusion — testing with genuine newcomers surfaces onboarding problems veterans have long since adapted around. The "kayak problem" concept is a useful filter for game UX bug triage: momentary menu confusion a player self-corrects from isn't the same severity as a dead-end players can't escape.

## Chapter: Mobile: It's not just a city in Alabama anymore

Mobile usability is framed as fundamentally the same principles under harsher constraints ("the tyranny of the itty-bitty living space"): less screen space forces harder content-priority tradeoffs, and Krug argues most serious mobile usability failures trace back to a poorly made tradeoff rather than to not caring. Key mobile-specific issues: affordances (visual cues suggesting how to interact with something) become harder to convey without hover states, which touchscreens don't support — flat/minimal visual design compounds this by removing exactly the multi-dimensional visual distinctions (depth, texture, borders) that signaled "this is tappable." Performance/speed matters more on mobile because connection quality is unpredictable, and slow loading reads to users as carelessness. For app usability specifically, Krug separates three attributes beyond baseline usability: delight (an app doing something surprising/impressive that feels almost magical), learnability (especially critical for apps with more than a handful of features, since most apps offer minimal onboarding and users won't hunt for help screens), and memorability (whether a once-learned interaction is still recalled on the next use session — an app that must be "relearned" every time it's opened is at high risk of abandonment, and "life is cheap [99 cents] on mobile," so the bar for tolerating friction is low).

**Game UX bridge**: Console/PC HUD and menu affordance design faces an analogous problem whenever games chase minimalist "clean" aesthetics — a flat, low-contrast icon set can look elegant in a style guide while failing the in-context "is this interactive?" test during actual play, especially under time pressure or on a controller with no cursor-hover equivalent. Krug's three extra app attributes translate cleanly to games: a delightful onboarding moment (a satisfying first-ability unlock) sells the game emotionally, but if the underlying system isn't learnable (players can't reconstruct how to use it without a tutorial replay) or memorable (they forget the input combo across a week-long break), delight alone won't retain players — this is the core argument for teaching mechanics through repeatable, discoverable interaction patterns rather than one-time tutorial popups.

## Chapter: Usability as common courtesy

Krug frames every user's tolerance as a finite, idiosyncratic, situational "reservoir of goodwill" that depletes with each frustration (hidden information like pricing or support contact, punishing rigid input formatting, requesting unnecessary personal data, insincere reassurance copy, unnecessary promotional friction, unprofessional appearance) and refills through considerate design choices (surfacing the information users actually need — especially in a crisis, per his airline-strike anecdote — saving the user steps, investing visibly in support/help content, maintaining genuinely useful and current FAQs, offering graceful error recovery, and apologizing honestly when a real limitation can't be fixed). The reservoir can occasionally be emptied by a single bad moment (e.g., an unreasonably long registration form), and some deliberately "unfriendly" choices can still be a legitimate business tradeoff as long as they're made knowingly rather than by accident.

**Game UX bridge**: This maps directly onto player-facing systems that feel adversarial rather than courteous — obscuring real-money prices behind multiple currency-conversion steps, punishing exact input formatting in usernames/codes, or padding onboarding with unnecessary account-linking prompts all drain the same "reservoir of goodwill" Krug describes, and the effect compounds: a player already frustrated by matchmaking will tolerate far less UI friction than one entering fresh. The "tell me what I want to know upfront" principle argues for surfacing patch-note-relevant information (nerfs, downtime, known issues) prominently rather than making players hunt external channels for it — Krug's airline-strike example (silence during a crisis is worse than bad news) is a close analogue to a game going quiet during a server outage or major balance controversy.

## Chapter: Accessibility and you

Krug argues accessibility is inseparable from usability (a site unusable by disabled users isn't fully usable), but that abstract statistics-based arguments ("X% of users have a disability") rarely persuade skeptical practitioners the way concrete, human-scale examples do ("blind users can now read almost any newspaper independently"). He identifies two real barriers beyond attitude: perceived extra workload, and designers' fear that accessible design necessarily means compromised/uglier design for everyone else (the "buttered cat" framing — forced conflict between two good goals) — countered by his Braille-taxi-sign example, where a well-designed solution serves both audiences without compromise to either. His concrete, prioritized action list: (1) fix the usability problems that confuse *everyone* first, since confusion is compounded, not created, by disability (this is the highest-leverage single action); (2) study how screen-reader users actually navigate (they "scan with their ears," listening to link/heading beginnings and skipping ahead the same way sighted users scan visually — reinforcing that front-loading key words at the start of labels matters even more for accessibility); (3) then apply concrete technical fixes — meaningful alt text, correctly nested heading levels, form labels tied to fields, skip-navigation links, full keyboard operability, sufficient color contrast.

**Game UX bridge**: "Fix what confuses everyone first" is the highest-value accessibility lesson for game UI — colorblind-unfriendly status effect icons, low-contrast subtitle text, and unlabeled interactive HUD elements are exactly the kind of universal-confusion issues that, once fixed, disproportionately help accessibility-dependent players without requiring specialized accommodation work. The "scan with ears" finding directly informs screen-reader/narrator support in menus: informative words (the item name, the action) need to load-bear at the *start* of a read-aloud label, not be buried after generic framing text. The "buttered cat" fallacy — assuming accessible and delightful/legible design are opposed — argues against treating colorblind modes, remappable controls, and adjustable text scaling as compromises; well-executed versions (distinguishable icon shapes, not just color, for status effects) improve legibility for every player, not just colorblind ones.

## Chapter: Guide for the perplexed

The closing chapter addresses organizational change: how to build support for usability practice inside a team/company that doesn't yet prioritize it. Krug's recommended tactics, in rough order of leverage: get decision-makers to *watch* a live usability test in person (far more persuasive than a report, because seeing a real user struggle is memorable in a way summarized findings aren't); run the very first test informally, on your own initiative and time, targeting an easy, cheaply-fixable problem so you can demonstrate a concrete before/after win; test a competitor's product, which is politically low-risk (nobody on your team has skin in the game) and still builds the case for testing as a practice; lead with empathy for management's constraints rather than crusading; and stay humble about your role rather than positioning yourself as bringing enlightenment to an unwashed team. He closes with an explicit ethical line: usability skills should be used to help people accomplish real goals, not to manipulate them into choices against their own interest (contrasting persuasion, which he's fine with, against dark-pattern deception, which he isn't) — and a short list of near-absolute rules: never combine small type with low contrast, avoid placeholder-only form labels that disappear on input, preserve the visited/unvisited link color distinction, and never let headings float ambiguously between the sections they separate.

**Game UX bridge**: "Get leadership to watch a real playtest" is arguably the single most effective lever a game UX/UI designer has for winning internal design arguments — a producer watching a real player get stuck in the crafting menu for three minutes changes priorities faster than any written report. Running a cheap, informal first playtest on a low-risk screen (a settings menu, not the core combat HUD) mirrors Krug's advice to build momentum with an easy, demonstrable win before asking for a bigger testing budget. The ethics line matters directly for monetization UI: Krug's distinction between legitimate persuasion (clear pricing, honest framing) and manipulation (confirm-shaming dialogs, disguised ads styled as content, timers designed to produce panic purchases) gives game UX designers language for pushing back on dark-pattern requests from monetization stakeholders.
