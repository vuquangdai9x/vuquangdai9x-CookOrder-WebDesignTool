## Level Design

**Core frames**

- **Lynch's five elements** (from urban planning): paths, edges, districts, nodes, landmarks. Almost all wayfinding problems are one of these missing — most often a landmark.
- **Pacing as a wave, not a ramp.** Sustained intensity reads as flat. Alternate tension and release; the release is what makes the next peak legible as a peak.
- **Greybox first, always.** Layout, sightlines, and timing get validated with no art. Art applied to a bad layout hides the problem and makes it expensive to fix.

**Heuristics**

- Teach in a safe room, test in a cheap room, punish in an expensive room. Introduce a mechanic where failure costs nothing before it costs something.
- Give the player a landmark visible from multiple districts — orientation is what stops "lost" from becoming "bored."
- Guide with light, contrast, and motion before resorting to arrows or waypoint markers.

**Practical: common cases**

- *"Players get lost."* — Add a landmark before adding a marker. Markers fix the symptom and remove the pleasure of navigating.
- *"This section drags."* — Check the pacing wave: it's usually too long at one intensity, not too long overall. Cut or vary the middle, not the ending.
- *"The level is fun for us but brutal for testers."* — Team skill is the problem. Re-tune from telemetry (death locations, retry counts), not from feel.
- *"We need more content fast."* — Modular/composable spaces plus variation rules scale far better than bespoke levels, but need the variation rules written down or everything reads identical.
- *"Where does this new mechanic go?"* — Safe room to learn, cheap room to test, expensive room to punish. If there's no safe room, the mechanic will read as unfair the first time it matters.

**Failure modes to flag**

- Difficulty tuned by the designer, who is the most practiced player alive at their own level.
- A "critical path" that's only obvious to someone who knows the layout.
- Set-pieces built before the core traversal/combat loop is proven — expensive content married to an unproven verb.

## UX

**Core frames**

- **Affordance / signifier / feedback** (Norman). An affordance is what an action *is* possible; a signifier is what *tells* the player it's possible; feedback confirms it happened. Most "confusing UI" complaints are a missing signifier, not a missing feature.
- **Cognitive load budget.** A player can track roughly 3–5 live variables under pressure. Every HUD element, resource, and timer spends from that budget. If a new system needs a new HUD element, something else should earn its place or go.
- **FTUE gradient.** Teach one thing at a time, in the moment it's first needed, through play rather than text. A tutorial screen is what you write when the design didn't teach itself.

**Heuristics**

- Anything the player must react to inside ~200 ms must be readable *peripherally* — silhouette, color, motion — not by reading text.
- If a first-time player hesitates for more than ~3 seconds on a screen, that screen has a signifier problem.
- Accessibility is cheapest at the start: don't encode critical state in color alone; allow remapping; caption meaningful audio cues. Retrofitting these late is where they get cut for scope.

**Practical: common cases**

- *"Players skip the tutorial."* — They always will. Move teaching into the first real encounter and design so the mechanic is discoverable by trying it, not by reading about it.
- *"The HUD is too cluttered."* — Rank elements by how often they're acted on, not by how often they change. Demote anything that's merely informative to on-demand.
- *"New players bounce, veterans love it."* — Usually a first-session legibility problem, not a depth problem. Instrument time-to-first-confusion before touching systems.
- *"We need a settings screen for this."* — A setting is often an unresolved design decision handed to the player. Fine for accessibility and preference; a smell when it's covering an unmade choice.

**Failure modes to flag**

- Information the player needs *now* placed where they must look away from the action.
- Tutorializing mechanics the player already understood from context (over-teaching erodes trust as fast as under-teaching).
- Onboarding tested only by people who already know the game.

**Ask the human**

Whether the intended feel is "immediately legible" vs. "deliberately opaque and discovered" — some games earn a lot from withholding clarity. That's a Human Domain call; don't optimize readability by default without asking.
