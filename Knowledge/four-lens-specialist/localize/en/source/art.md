## Animation Design

**Core frames**

- **The classical principles** (Thomas & Johnston): anticipation, squash/stretch, follow-through, ease-in/out, exaggeration, staging, arcs, timing. Timing and anticipation matter most for game feel; the rest are polish.
- **Game animation is interruptible animation.** Film animation plays out; game animation must be cancellable, blendable, and responsive. A gorgeous 40-frame windup that can't be cancelled is a control problem, not an art asset.
- **Game feel** (Swink): input → response → context → polish. Animation sits at the response layer and is where most "feels sluggish" complaints actually originate.

**Heuristics**

- Keep input-to-visible-response under ~100 ms. If the full animation is longer, start it with an immediate, readable first frame.
- Anticipation frames sell weight but cost responsiveness — heavier actions can afford more; core traversal can't.
- Readability at gameplay speed beats beauty at 25% speed. Review animation in-context at real speed, not in an animation viewer.

**Practical: common cases**

- *"Controls feel sluggish."* — Check input-to-first-visible-frame before blaming the animation length. Start the response instantly and let the rest play out behind it.
- *"Hits don't feel impactful."* — Impact is mostly hit-stop, screen-shake, audio, and a strong pose — not more animation frames. Add feedback layers before re-animating.
- *"The character snaps between states."* — Missing transitions or blend windows. Cheaper fix: add short transition poses on the most common state pairs only.
- *"Enemy attacks feel unfair."* — Tells are too short, too similar to each other, or occluded. Make the anticipation pose distinct in silhouette and lengthen it before nerfing damage.

**Failure modes to flag**

- Animation length dictating gameplay timing rather than the reverse.
- Enemy tells too short or visually inconsistent to read as fair.
- Idle/transition states missing, making characters snap between poses.

## Art Direction

**Core frames**

- **A direction is a set of constraints, not a mood board.** "Painterly, warm, low-contrast, no pure blacks, silhouette-driven" is directable. "Stylized and cool" is not.
- **Cohesion beats fidelity.** A consistent, cheaper style will read as more professional than an inconsistent, expensive one. This is the single biggest quality lever available to a small team.
- **Color scripting.** Plan the color/value progression across the whole experience so emotional beats have contrast to land against — borrowed directly from film.

**Heuristics**

- Write the style guide as rules that can be violated knowingly: palette, value range, shape language, line weight, level of detail, lighting logic.
- Every new asset request should name which existing asset it must sit next to. Cohesion breaks at the seams between separately-briefed assets.
- Defend the direction against feature-by-feature erosion: each individual exception is reasonable; the accumulation is what kills identity.

**Practical: common cases**

- *"Assets from different artists don't match."* — The style guide isn't specific enough. Specify palette, value range, line weight, detail density, and lighting logic with example assets — adjectives alone never converge.
- *"We can't afford the art we want."* — Pick a style whose cost scales with your team: strong silhouettes, flat/limited palettes, and bold shapes carry further per hour than detailed rendering.
- *"A new feature doesn't fit the look."* — Decide explicitly whether the direction bends or the feature adapts, then record it. Unlogged exceptions are how a direction quietly dies.
- *"How do we brief an outsourcer?"* — Reference assets, the specific rules, and the neighbouring asset it must sit beside. Never brief on mood words alone.

**Ask the human**

The direction itself — always. The agent's job is to check consistency *against* a stated direction, and to flag when a new asset or marketing beat contradicts it.

## UI / Visual Design

**Core frames**

- **Visual hierarchy.** Size, contrast, position, and isolation determine what gets seen first. If everything is emphasized, nothing is. Rank every screen's elements 1–3 and check the design actually reflects that ranking.
- **Grid and rhythm.** Consistent spacing scale and alignment do more for perceived quality than any single asset. Irregular spacing reads as "unfinished" even when the art is good.
- **Contrast for function, color for meaning.** Legibility comes from value (light/dark) contrast; hue carries semantic meaning (danger, healing, faction). Test in greyscale — if it falls apart, it was relying on hue for hierarchy.

**Heuristics**

- Target the smallest supported screen first; a HUD that works on a phone works on a monitor, rarely the reverse.
- Keep interactive touch targets generous (≈44 px minimum on mobile) and keep critical readouts out of thumb-occluded zones.
- Diegetic UI (health shown on the character/world) buys immersion but costs legibility. That trade is a deliberate choice, not a default.

**Practical: common cases**

- *"The UI looks amateurish but I can't say why."* — Usually inconsistent spacing and too many type sizes. Impose a spacing scale and cap yourself at 3 type sizes and 2 weights before redrawing anything.
- *"Players miss important alerts."* — Motion and position beat size and color for attracting attention. Move it into the gaze path (near the action) rather than making it bigger in a corner.
- *"It's unreadable on small screens."* — Design at the smallest target first, then scale up. Retro-shrinking a desktop HUD never works.
- *"The capsule/icon isn't converting."* — Test at actual thumbnail size against competitors on a shelf, not in isolation at full resolution.

**Failure modes to flag**

- Text over busy backgrounds without a scrim or outline.
- State communicated by hue alone (fails for colorblind players and in poor lighting).
- Store/capsule art designed at full size and never checked as a thumbnail.
