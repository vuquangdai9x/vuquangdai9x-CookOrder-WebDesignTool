# A Pattern Language: Towns, Buildings, Construction — Lookup Summary

## How the Language Works
The book is 253 numbered "patterns" ordered largest-to-smallest (region/town, neighborhood, building, room, construction detail). Every pattern follows a fixed format: a link to the larger patterns it helps complete, a problem backed by observation/data, a boldface instruction, a diagram, and a link down to the smaller patterns that complete it — no pattern is meant to stand alone. Patterns carry two asterisks (near-universal invariant), one asterisk (good but improvable), or none (one possible solution among many).
→ see learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § How the Language Works

## Gradients of Publicness and Neighborhood Character
People split along an extrovert/introvert axis in how close to activity they want to live; a cited survey found roughly 20% want to be near a commercial center, 60% a middle distance, 20% far away. The pattern calls for offering three genuinely different address types (near-action, in-between, remote) within any cluster, each served by a matching path character — busy and wide near services, narrow and twisting in quiet zones.
→ see learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Gradients of Publicness and Neighborhood Character

## Landmarks, Gateways, and Orientation
Main gateways should be built as solid, deliberately noticeable "things," with the passage through them marked by a change in light, level, or surface. High Places addresses the near-universal instinct to climb to a vantage point and to orient toward a distant landmark from the ground — every hamlet tends to produce a dominating landmark that does both jobs at once.
→ see learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Landmarks, Gateways, and Orientation

## Public Greens and Small Squares: Human-Scale Thresholds
Park usage drops off sharply beyond a roughly three-minute walk (~750 ft) from home, an S-shaped distance-decay curve with a hard threshold rather than a gradual falloff. Square sizing is grounded in measured limits of face-to-face communication (comfortable conversation up to ~75 ft, hopeless past ~100 ft), and plaza "liveliness" is shown empirically to track people-per-square-foot: around 150 sq ft/person reads alive, past ~500 sq ft/person a plaza reads dead regardless of design quality.
→ see learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Public Greens and Small Squares: Human-Scale Thresholds

## Site Repair and Positive Outdoor Space
Buildings should go on the worst part of a site, not the best, to avoid destroying what's already good and to give the poor remainder some use. Positive Outdoor Space argues people instinctively avoid the fully open middle of a space and seek partial enclosure, so the gaps between buildings need to be shaped as deliberate, convex, partially-enclosed volumes rather than leftover L-shaped voids.
→ see learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Site Repair and Positive Outdoor Space

## Entrances, Gateways, and Transitions
Main entrances should be visible from an acute-angle approach by projecting past the building line or raising height locally. Half-Hidden Garden resolves a placement paradox (front gardens are too exposed to use, back gardens too isolated) by placing gardens to the side with a thin sightline back to the street. Entrance Transition argues people carry "street behavior" that must be broken by a sensory shift (light, level, surface — a cited study used a bright carpet) before they can relax into an interior space.
→ see learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Entrances, Gateways, and Transitions

## Paths, Arcades, and the Edges of Public Space
People walk by picking a visible intermediate goal and re-picking a new one roughly every hundred yards, so paths should be built by placing goals first, then connecting them, with goals no more than a few hundred feet apart. A public space's life gathers at its edge, not its open middle — the edge must be "scalloped" with pause-points around the *entire* perimeter or the space's liveliness collapses; a slightly elevated, easily reachable spot (like low wide steps) is the most naturally occupied kind of seat.
→ see learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Paths, Arcades, and the Edges of Public Space

## The Intimacy Gradient and Interior Flow
Every building needs rooms sequenced by degree of privacy (public entrance → semi-private common areas → private rooms) so that physical position communicates trust level without words — demonstrated with a Peruvian household where fiesta guests self-sort by how far into the house they're permitted. Common Areas at the Heart adds that a shared gathering space only gets used if daily circulation paths run tangent to it (close enough to notice, not so direct it stays too exposed to settle in).
→ see learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § The Intimacy Gradient and Interior Flow

## Light as Navigation and Mood
People are described as phototropic, drifting toward the brightest visible point even when not moving with intent, so entrances and circulation nodes must be the brightest spots or foot traffic gets misdirected. A cited lounge study found occupants disproportionately chose seats next to lamps even when overall light was already sufficient for reading, supporting deliberately uneven "pools" of warm light over gathering points rather than flat illumination. A single framed glimpse of a spectacular view at a threshold point stays powerful indefinitely, while a view left constantly visible fades into unnoticed background.
→ see learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Light as Navigation and Mood

## Windows, Views, and Small Gathering Places
Office-worker studies cited in the text found people prefer windows with meaningful views of activity over merely large but visually empty ones. A loose, slightly-overcrowded circular seating arrangement anchored by a fireplace or similar focal point stays socially "alive" better than a rigid formal layout. A small outdoor-bench survey found every occupied bench simultaneously had a view of activity, direct sun, and low wind, while none of the empty ones had all three together.
→ see learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Windows, Views, and Small Gathering Places

## Construction and Ornament
The final third of the book covers buildable structural detail (framing, openings, wall construction) below level-design scale, but closes with finishing patterns worth reusing conceptually: warm color palettes, deliberately varied/mismatched furniture as a value rather than a flaw, uneven "pools" of light, and filling a finished space with an occupant's real, meaningful possessions rather than generic decor.
→ see learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Construction and Ornament

## Key Takeaways for Level Design
- Design nested, not flat: every space should be justified by the larger space it sits inside and completed by the smaller spaces within it → learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § How the Language Works
- Zone hubs by a felt publicness gradient with matching path character per zone, rather than uniform corridors → learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Gradients of Publicness and Neighborhood Character
- Give every zone a landmark for orientation and an elevated vantage point players can climb to → learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Landmarks, Gateways, and Orientation
- Size gathering spaces by expected occupant density, not editor scale, and seed rest points at a walkable threshold distance → learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Public Greens and Small Squares: Human-Scale Thresholds
- Shape the empty space between structures as deliberately as the structures themselves, and site landmarks on unremarkable terrain to preserve striking natural spots → learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Site Repair and Positive Outdoor Space
- Mark every hub-to-high-stakes-area transition with a genuine sensory break to reset player mode → learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Entrances, Gateways, and Transitions
- Chain paths through visible intermediate goals, and scallop plaza edges completely rather than partially → learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Paths, Arcades, and the Edges of Public Space
- Make physical depth into a space read as social/narrative depth, and place hubs tangent to through-traffic → learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § The Intimacy Gradient and Interior Flow
- Use uneven "pool" lighting to mark destinations and ration big views to a single framed glimpse at a threshold → learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Light as Navigation and Mood
- A usable rest spot needs sightline, comfort, and shelter together — any one alone tends to go unused → learned/alexander-pattern-language.md § Windows, Views, and Small Gathering Places
